## Abstract

Recurring narratives across cultures raise the possibility of shared ancestry, but similarity alone cannot distinguish common descent from borrowing or independent invention. This paper presents a source-first framework for investigating narrative relationships without presuming a universal origin, and reports what three independent computational instruments — a lexical retriever, a structural comparison engine, and a divine-concept state coder — do and do not show when that framework is applied. Attested versions are decomposed into source-bound events, participants, motifs, and outcomes; descriptive similarity is separated from linguistic and historical evidence; and proposed relationships are tested against dependent witnesses, generic motifs, alternative codings, and competing models.

A cleaned 65-work English-translation corpus (23,385 eligible passages, 8.73 million words) was used as a retrieval instrument. Of 9,428 cross-work candidate pairs, 8,677 (92.0%) join two works already linked by a configured source-dependence rule; excluding editorial apparatus and sub-threshold passages reduces the unfiltered candidate set by roughly two-thirds, while validation shows the same rules excluded seven annotated evidence passages, so retrieval recall under cleaning is unproven. A second engine compares coded narrative structure: 14 versions in four comparison classes, 94 events carrying 218 participant role assertions, 76 causal links and 38 preregistered motifs, scored as separate event-sequence, participant-role, causal, motif and symbol components across 91 version pairs rather than a blended percentage. Known dependent witnesses rank above every predicted-unrelated pair (minimum 0.632 against maximum 0.045), and re-coding every participant name leaves all 91 pair scores unchanged. Two results cut against the expectations of this research tradition: the sequence score is almost insensitive to event order (mean drop 0.0186 under permutation, below a preregistered 0.02 floor, for arithmetic reasons identified rather than tuned away), and structural and lexical similarity correlate only weakly and without established significance (Spearman ρ = 0.264; a quadratic-assignment permutation test on version labels gives two-tailed p = 0.065, so the textbook p = 0.019 rested on an independence the data do not have). The paper therefore abandons the composite similarity score, emits no ancestry estimate, and treats independent invention and unresolved relationships as substantive outcomes. A third instrument tests the precondition of this project's own motive claim — that “god” names one concept worldwide, so that its recurrence would indicate one invention. Its first version, a vocabulary census of ten analyst-authored divine-concept grammars, reported that no grammar was attested in all 65 works and read that as showing the motive claim has no stable referent. That inference is withdrawn here and is not a result of this paper: the census measured one generation's English translation vocabulary, and non-detection in such a corpus is predicted equally by every competing model of inheritance, so it discriminates between none of them. The census's companion finding did not survive an audit either. Its 46 machine matches for exclusivist oneness, published as the doctrine attested in four works across four traditions, become 51 once Gutenberg's hard line-wraps are collapsed; all 9 matches in the *Sacred Books of the East* volume sit inside its “Selections from the Koran”, 8 of them in a reprint of a Qur'an translation this corpus already counts and 1 in Carlyle's essay describing the formula, so the volume adds no independent witness; the corpus's `quran_sale` file prints Rodwell's name on its title page, not Sale's, so the work id and its configured dependence rule both misattribute the translation; 2 further matches are substring collisions, one of them the only match credited to the Book of Mormon. What survives is 34 confessional matches in two independent works, and 79 occurrences of 16 oneness formula classes the census never carried — 5 of them, over 3 distinct verses, the boast of a non-divine claimant (Isaiah 47:8, 47:10, Zephaniah 2:15), which a widened regex would count as doctrine. The census is therefore superseded by an instrument that codes divine-concept *states* instead of term vocabularies: twelve dimensions built to be orthogonal — ontological category; three separate plurality questions (how many beings a proposition acknowledges, whether the others are real, whether worship of them is permitted); agency; cosmic function; relation to the universe; temporal nature; and four separate interpretation questions (who speaks, what speech act, what discourse genre, what epistemic commitment) — applied proposition by proposition rather than passage by passage. It emits 47,641 candidate records over 36,775 propositions in 34,584 utterance windows, covering the same 23,385 eligible passages and 8.73 million words, and answers three questions per record separately: whether the concept is represented, whether the voice the text itself endorses affirms it, and whether it was historically transmitted. The 54 matches the previous version refused outright are therefore retained as conceptual evidence whose endorsement is withheld, not deleted; 22,818 records carry machine-classified affirmative doctrine and none is human-verified. `historical_transmission` is `not_yet_assessable` in all 47,641 records, because composition, witness and translation dates are separate fields and none is filled, and 9 dimension values are published as undetectable by this instrument rather than absent. Its candidate graph now ships all 538,529 passage pairs the MinHash banding produced against a naive space of 98,497,630 — where the earlier build shipped 149 edges after a top-100 truncation — and measures rather than asserts its own coverage: recall against exhaustive comparison is 0.0232 on a deterministic 1,632-passage stratified subset, so the published edge set is a blocking sample and not a complete map. A blinded annotation packet of 4,699 propositions over 48 value strata, 65 works and 8 speech acts, with a separate answer key and a published codebook, is ready for two independent coders; no coding has been performed, so this repository reports no κ, no precision and no recall for the coder-facing instrument. That frame ships as a methodology with four competing historical models, priced operator derivations, licence conditions on reporting them, and the observations that would separate them ([TRACK_B_METHOD_v2.md](../../TRACK_B_METHOD_v2.md)), not as an answer to them: the model-comparison layer's own output is `not_yet_testable`, listing the dated attestations and derivation records it is missing, and asserts no historical conclusion. The further claim that no such being exists remains outside the reach of any textual method, in either direction. Global comparison is defensible as candidate discovery and falsification; reconstruction must be restricted to elements supported by explicit historical models, and 194 unit tests plus a further 7 parameterised subtests and the result validators enforce those boundaries.

## 1. Introduction

Recurring stories and motifs are conspicuous across human cultures. Storm beings confront serpentine adversaries; floods destroy or renew human communities; primordial bodies become parts of the cosmos; heroes descend among the dead. Such resemblances invite a historical question: did particular narratives descend from shared ancestors, travel between communities, or arise independently under similar constraints?

The question is legitimate, but its most ambitious answers are often methodologically weak. A collection of similarities cannot distinguish inheritance from borrowing, and neither can it distinguish either from convergence. Broad labels such as “flood myth,” “dying god,” or “dragon-slayer” can conceal differences in agents, actions, sequence, function, and outcome. The problem is therefore not simply to discover parallels. It is to determine which parallels require a historical explanation, which explanations are compatible with the evidence, and where reconstruction must stop.

This paper proposes and applies a source-first comparative framework. Its primary objects are attested narrative versions, not religions treated as unified systems. It separates descriptive resemblance from historical relationship, composition from attestation, and reconstruction from conjecture. It also treats independent invention and unresolved relationship as substantive outcomes rather than failed comparisons.

The analysis addresses four questions:

1. Can narrative similarities be coded without erasing culturally specific meanings?
2. Do shared structures provide evidence of common descent or transmission once generic motifs and dependent sources are controlled?
3. Can competing historical models be tested against contradictory evidence and appropriate null cases?
4. What elements, if any, can be reconstructed without inventing an ancestral story?

For each specified element or event alignment, the study compares inheritance, convergence, contact, and mixed-history models using preregistered differential predictions. Whether those elements form one or multiple families is assessed separately. A universal-origin model is neither assumed nor tested by this pilot; it would require its own scope, predictions, and evidence.

## 2. Concepts and competing explanations

A **motif** is a recurring element, whereas a **narrative** is an ordered and causally connected sequence involving participants and events. A **tradition** is a historically situated body of transmitted material associated with communities, languages, and institutions. A **religion** may include narratives but also practices, ethics, institutions, and theological claims. Similarity between narratives therefore does not establish equivalence between religions.

A **narrative version** denotes the particular sequence attested in an identified passage, performance, inscription, or iconographic context. This distinction matters because a figure may appear in several stories that cannot legitimately be combined into one synthetic “myth.” Likewise, a late record directly attests the story at its recording date, but its earlier history requires separate evidence.

Four process hypotheses are evaluated for each specified element or alignment:

- **H1, common descent:** shared material was inherited from an ancestral narrative tradition.
- **H2, independent invention:** similar features emerged independently through recurring experiences, cognitive dispositions, ecological conditions, or narrative constraints.
- **H3, transmission:** material was borrowed directly or indirectly between historically connected communities.
- **H4, mixed history:** inheritance, borrowing, innovation, and loss operated together.

A separate structural question asks whether the versions form one or multiple families. These possibilities may apply to different elements of the same narrative. A borrowed episode can be integrated into an inherited cosmology; a widespread motif can receive an independently invented sequence. Models that make identical predictions for the surviving evidence remain unresolved.

## 3. Materials and methods

### 3.1 Source and dating protocol

Primary witnesses, critical editions, original-language texts, and transparent scholarly translations take priority. Peer-reviewed historical, linguistic, archaeological, and anthropological research supplies context and competing interpretations. Modern retellings and general websites are discovery aids, not evidence for historical claims.

Every material observation requires an exact passage, tablet, hymn, chapter, line, or section locator. Original wording and consequential translation choices must be inspected where accessible. For oral and Indigenous material, recording context, collector, community authorization, and restrictions form part of the evidence.

Four dates are distinguished: composition of an expression, production of a surviving witness, recording of a performance, and inferred age of the narrative. These dates may differ substantially. A late manuscript can preserve an early composition; a late ethnographic record can preserve older oral material without thereby proving great antiquity.

### 3.2 Narrative decomposition

Each version is represented at three levels:

1. **Attested description:** source wording, participants, actions, order, context, and outcome.
2. **Comparative coding:** versioned motif, role, symbol, and event categories with explicit definitions.
3. **Historical interpretation:** proposed relationships, transformations, and reconstructions linked to supporting and contradicting evidence.

Source-native descriptions are retained alongside comparative codes. Thompson’s motif index and the ATU tale-type system aid discovery where applicable, but neither supplies a complete global ontology (Thompson 1955–1958; Uther 2004). Character identity is separated from narrative role, so shared storm associations do not make two figures identical. Alternative segmentations and disputed codings remain available for sensitivity analysis.

### 3.3 Similarity without ancestry by fiat

Descriptive similarity is calculated independently of linguistic and transmission evidence, and it is reported as separate components rather than blended. The index originally specified for this framework was

\[
S_{desc}=100(0.40S_{structure}+0.25S_{motif}+0.20S_{role}+0.15S_{symbol}),
\]

where each component is normalized between zero and one, structure uses explicit event alignment, motifs and symbols use weighted overlap, and roles use participant matching based on actions and relations. That composite is **not used** in the implemented protocol (Section 5.8). Averaging components measured on different assessed domains would require a missing component to be imputed or silently renormalised, and it would destroy the pattern that matters: a pair can share most of its adjudicated motifs while aligning almost no events, or the reverse. The component weights were research-design choices subject to sensitivity analysis, not validated historical constants, and the sensitivity analysis confirms that the ordering is bound to alignment parameters rather than to the weights.

These scores answer only “How similar are these coded versions?” They do not answer “How probable is common ancestry?” Linguistic relationship, chronological feasibility, documented contact, verbal correspondence, shared errors, distribution, and contradictory evidence are assessed separately. Numerical historical probabilities are admissible only under a calibrated model.

### 3.4 Controls and falsification

A global comparison is vulnerable to selection bias because investigators can find some shared motif among almost any sufficiently large pair of corpora. The framework therefore uses positive controls with independently supported relationships, negative and matched controls, and narrative categories not used to design the target comparison.

Null models must preserve motif prevalence, co-occurrence, genre, period, corpus size, language-family clustering, and dependent witnesses. Unconstrained random pairings would exaggerate significance. Confirmatory tests are preregistered, report effect sizes and uncertainty, and correct for multiple comparisons. Results are retested under alternative translations, annotations, weights, and exclusion of late or dependent sources.

Phylogenetic studies of folktales demonstrate both the analytical potential and the model dependence of historical inference from narrative distributions (Tehrani 2013; da Silva and Tehrani 2016). Their methods do not license transferring their conclusions to unrelated narratives without new evidence.

A proposed historical relationship is weakened when its distinctive shared sequence reduces to a generic motif, when its chronology is incompatible, when apparent independent witnesses are textually dependent, or when a preregistered contact or convergence model fits held-out evidence better. Repeated failure of a single-origin model across independent panels would limit or reject that model without pretending to prove that no remote historical connection ever existed.

### 3.5 Reconstruction rule

Support for a historical relationship does not by itself identify ancestral features. Ancestral states require an explicit genealogy or network and a model of transformation, loss, and borrowing. Shared elements are not assembled into fluent new prose. **Attested** describes evidence in surviving witnesses; proposed ancestral elements are labeled **comparatively reconstructed**, **conjectural**, or **unrecoverable**. Competing models and unexplained elements are reported together.

## 4. Pilot design

The pilot uses three panels to prevent the analytical vocabulary from being tailored to one desired relationship. Panel A examines six combat narratives or proposed narrative groupings: Indra–Vritra, the Hittite Storm God–Illuyanka, Zeus–Typhon, Marduk–Tiamat, Thor–Jörmungandr, and Perun–Veles. They are candidates for comparison, not presumed descendants of one story. Panel B compares selected flood, destruction, and renewal narratives from Mesopotamian, Hebrew, and geographically distant traditions. Panel C compares creation through bodily division in the Purusha, Ymir, and Pangu materials.

The panels test different evidentiary problems. Panel A asks whether an apparent “storm god versus serpent” category survives event-level comparison. Panel B separates regional transmission questions from the global distribution of flood stories and from the problem of late recording. Panel C tests whether a broad shared theme also contains a specific shared sequence and body-to-cosmos mapping.

No version is merged with another episode involving the same figure. In particular, Thor’s fishing encounter and final conflict with Jörmungandr are distinct attestations. Nor is a modern scholarly reconstruction treated as equivalent to a surviving narrative: this distinction is essential for Perun–Veles.

## 5. Pilot analysis and computational results

### 5.1 Combat with a serpentine or primordial adversary

The first panel does not support treating its six candidates as interchangeable versions of one story. The most consequential distinction is between a shared combat motif and a shared event sequence.

| Attested version | Primary textual anchor | Attested structure relevant to comparison | Descriptive differences limiting direct event alignment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indra–Vritra | *Rigveda* 1.32 | Indra strikes Vritra with the vajra; obstructed waters are released. | The water-release outcome cannot be transferred automatically to other combat stories. |
| Illuyanka, first version | Hittite Illuyanka myth, CTH 321 | Initial defeat, recovery through intermediaries, and a later killing of Illuyanka. | Initial defeat and recovery complicate alignment with a one-sided victory. |
| Illuyanka, second version | Hittite Illuyanka myth, CTH 321 | Inaras and Hupasiyas assist the Storm God; a feast and binding precede the killing. | The intermediary, feast, and binding sequence is distinct. |
| Zeus–Typhon | Hesiod, *Theogony* 820–868 | Zeus confronts the many-headed Typhon with thunder and lightning, overcomes him, and casts him into Tartarus. | Genealogy, setting, and outcome differ; blocked-water release is absent. |
| Marduk–Tiamat | *Enuma Elish*, especially Tablets IV–V | Marduk defeats Tiamat and her coalition, then divides her body in the creation of cosmic order. | Tiamat is not merely a serpent; corpse division is the decisive outcome. |
| Thor’s fishing encounter | *Hymiskviða* 21–25; *Gylfaginning* 48 | Thor draws Jörmungandr up on a fishing line during the encounter with Hymir. | This episode must not be merged with Ragnarok. |
| Thor’s final battle | *Gylfaginning* 51 | Thor kills the serpent but dies from its poison after nine steps. | Mutual destruction is not water release or creation from the opponent’s body. |

Perun–Veles is excluded from this table of surviving versions because no directly attested pagan narrative equivalent to these texts is supplied. Its proposed combat belongs in a separate reconstruction case; using the reconstruction as another independent witness would be circular.

One narrower candidate alignment for future investigation would include an obstructing adversary, divine weapon, ordered combat, and water release. This pilot neither establishes that it is the strongest alignment nor demonstrates that it was inherited. Removing or changing the last event changes the historical question, but descriptive difference alone does not disprove common descent: a model must specify which features it expects to remain conserved.

This does not eliminate historical relationships among the Eurasian materials. Comparative Indo-European research provides a disciplined precedent for investigating inherited poetic and mythological material, while its reconstructions remain incomplete and debated (Watkins 1995; West 2007). Anatolian and Near Eastern contacts make borrowing and mixed histories serious alternatives. A narrative family must be defined by shared innovations under an explicit model, not by a modern category that combines different outcomes.

### 5.2 Flood, destruction, and renewal

The Mesopotamian flood account in *Gilgamesh* XI and Genesis 6–9 contain much more specific correspondences than the bare occurrence of a catastrophic flood: a chosen survivor, construction of a vessel, preservation of life, grounding, and bird dispatch form part of the comparison. Their historical relationship must nevertheless be investigated through particular texts, languages, chronologies, and regional transmission, not through the general category “flood myth.”

A distant flood episode does not automatically add another independent descendant of the same story. The flood in the *Popol Vuh*, for example, belongs to a sequence of unsuccessful creations and the destruction of the wooden people. Its agents, victims, purpose, and outcome differ from the survival narratives above. The early-modern recording and transmission history of the *Popol Vuh* also form part of the evidence; its narrative cannot be assigned an unsupported primordial date merely because it records Indigenous tradition.

The methodological result is that “flood” is too coarse to serve as the principal historical unit. Ordered event combinations and their cultural functions provide a stronger comparison, while ecological plausibility and later religious contact remain live alternative explanations.

### 5.3 Creation through bodily division

*Rigveda* 10.90 presents Purusha in a sacrificial account whose divisions generate cosmic and social categories. In *Gylfaginning*, Ymir is killed and his flesh, blood, bones, and skull furnish materials for the created world. The Pangu tradition likewise associates a primordial body with cosmic transformation, but its textual history and narrative sequence must be established from early attestations rather than modern summaries.

The shared proposition “the world derives from a primordial body” is significant as a recurring motif. It is not yet a shared ancestral narrative. A defensible comparison must ask who acts, whether sacrifice or killing occurs, which body parts map to which cosmic elements, in what order, and what social or theological work the account performs. Different answers can reduce an apparently exact parallel to a broad conceptual resemblance.

### 5.4 Scope of the pilot results

This illustrative source-critical pilot identifies descriptive heterogeneity in selected cases and motivates event-level testing. It does not estimate how often motif groupings fail, discriminate among historical models, or test universal origin. Some candidates justify deeper regional investigation; Perun–Veles instead demonstrates the need to distinguish surviving narratives from scholarly reconstructions.

The same panels were later coded and scored, so the qualitative claims above became measurements rather than assertions (Section 5.8). Indra–Vṛtra against Typhon returns \(S_{sequence}\) 0.221, Thor's fishing episode against Indra–Vṛtra 0.156, and Ymir against Pangu 0.154 — none of them the high figure a "same story" category would predict. The motif component is thinner still and must be read with its domain: 0.412 for Indra–Vṛtra–Typhon over two adjudicated motifs, 0.000 for Thor's fishing against Indra–Vṛtra over two, and 0.250 for Ymir–Pangu over two. A score computed over two motifs is arithmetic, not evidence, which is why the validator publishes domain size and flags every such pair. The water-release and corpse-division distinctions that §§5.1 and 5.3 raise by inspection are therefore exactly what the instrument fails to find shared, and the agreement between the reading and the measurement is a consistency check rather than an independent confirmation: the same analyst produced both.

Accordingly, no global similarity percentage or ancestral story is reported. Independent event-level annotation, control scoring, and source-critical investigation remain necessary before historical conclusions. A reconstruction that cannot survive those steps should be abandoned, not made more imaginative.

### 5.5 Computational baseline experiment

A reproducible baseline was run on 42 English-language works and translations comprising 7,549,234 words. Hindu material included 13 works and 3,846,519 words, making the snapshot intentionally useful for pipeline testing but imbalanced as a global sample. Fixed 350-word passages were compared using TF-IDF with unigrams and bigrams. The run retained 9,648 cross-work candidate pairs; 18,963 random cross-work pairs had mean similarity 0.0141 and a 99th percentile of 0.0512. All source hashes and 21,580 passage offsets were validated, and repeated runs produced byte-identical result files.

Known-overlap controls were recovered: a Dhammapada passage shared between two Gutenberg sources scored 0.9119, while a Qur’an anthology overlap scored 0.6022. Manual inspection also identified direct Bible–Book of Mormon dependence and repeated Mahābhārata material. These results support use of the method for detecting textual reuse.

The false matches are more important for model criticism. The strongest inspected Hindu–Biblical pair, scoring 0.5254, occurred because editorial commentary in the Rāmāyaṇa volume directly quoted 1 Samuel. A Rāmāyaṇa–Poetic Edda pair consisted of index pages. A Popol Vuh–secondary synthesis pair could not be treated as independent corroboration. Such cases demonstrate the need for apparatus filtering and a source-dependence graph before comparative interpretation.

The baseline also failed as a measure of narrative order. Original passages compared with word-shuffled versions of themselves retained mean similarity 0.6740 in the main run. A unigram-only diagnostic scored 0.9940, while a bigram-only model scored 0.0896; the latter is sensitive to local wording but still does not encode event structure. Consequently, the experiment validates provenance and candidate retrieval, not historical or structural inference. Full configuration and review records are preserved in the accompanying test report and machine-readable results.

### 5.6 Relevance formulation and graph semantics

The candidate-retrieval score is explicit. For term \(t\) in passage \(d\), the implementation uses sublinear term frequency and smoothed inverse document frequency,

\[
tf(t,d)=1+\ln count(t,d),\qquad idf(t)=\ln\frac{1+N}{1+df(t)}+1.
\]

After English stop-word removal, unigram and bigram extraction, feature capping, and L2 normalization, lexical relevance is the cosine

\[
R_{lex}(p,q)=\frac{v_p\cdot v_q}{\lVert v_p\rVert\,\lVert v_q\rVert}.
\]

A passage pair enters the candidate set at \(R_{lex}\geq0.15\), with at most six cross-work neighbors retained per passage. For a work-level review ranking, the strongest eligible passage pair is used:

\[
R_{work}(A,B)=\max_{p\in A,q\in B}R_{lex}(p,q).
\]

This maximum is a triage heuristic, not a work-level historical score. Longer works provide more opportunities for a high maximum, and repeated candidates within one work pair are not independent observations. Random and length-matched controls estimate how unusual scores are under limited sampling models; they do not estimate the probability of transmission or ancestry.

The relevance graph is therefore read as `RETRIEVED_AS_CANDIDATE`, not as a resemblance verdict: an edge records only that two works produced at least one passage pair above the retrieval threshold under one fixed vector-space model. Known translation, anthology, compilation, and retelling relationships are marked and separated from the discovery ranking. An unmarked pair means "not covered by the current dependence rules," not "proved independent." No `DESCENDS_FROM` or `BORROWS_FROM` edge is inferred from cosine similarity.

Graph output is split into five layers so that premises, measurements and claims cannot be confused: documented source dependence between works; lexical retrieval candidates; structural comparison of annotated narrative versions (`COMPARED_WITH`, Section 5.8); adjudicated historical relationships, which this build leaves empty; and historical context attributes such as dating, language and access cautions. Every edge carries its own `edge_kind`, and a validator rejects a graph in which a retrieval edge has been relabelled as a resemblance, in which a comparison has been published as a verdict, or in which an adjudicated relation appears without adjudication. `RESEMBLES` is deliberately reserved: the builder records a promotion criterion — independently attested rather than merely unmarked witnesses, a shared comparison class, every nonzero component sitting on an assessed domain, and survival under the parameter sweeps — and no pair in this build satisfies it, so the published graph contains zero `RESEMBLES` edges.

The single-number descriptive formulation sketched in the protocol,

\[
S_{desc}=100(0.40S_{structure}+0.25S_{motif}+0.20S_{role}+0.15S_{symbol}),
\]

is **not computed**. Its components are now computed separately (Section 5.8), and that is precisely why the composite is abandoned: the components disagree, sometimes violently, and averaging them would decide the argument in advance instead of reporting it. A pair can share half its adjudicated motif inventory while aligning almost no events, or align events tightly while sharing no adjudicated motif. Which of those is true matters historically; a single number would destroy the information. Historical confidence remains a separate ordinal evidence assessment, never a transformation of a similarity formula.

### 5.7 Cleaned global corpus and dependence filtering (v2)

The 42-work baseline was replaced by a cleaned, broader snapshot: 65 works across 14 region labels and 20 source roles, 9,568,271 source words, of which 8,731,101 words in 23,385 eligible passages entered retrieval. Front matter, footnotes, appendices, editorial apparatus and passages below the minimum length were excluded before indexing rather than filtered afterwards. Passage counts and the full configuration are recorded in `results/v2_run_summary.json`.

Retrieval produced 9,428 cross-work candidate pairs. **8,677 of them (92.0%) join two works already linked by a configured source-dependence rule**, leaving 751 pairs not known dependent. That ratio is the most consequential number in the lexical experiment: at this corpus size, near-verbatim English wording is overwhelmingly an artifact of reuse, repetition and shared editions, not a signal waiting to be interpreted. Random cross-work pairs (n = 20,000) had mean similarity 0.0121 and 99th percentile 0.0416; length-matched pairs (mean absolute word-count difference 11.8) reached 0.0126 and 0.0426. Known-overlap positive controls behaved as expected, scoring 0.6000 (Qur'an text shared between two anthology volumes) and 0.6166 (Dhammapada text repeated in *Sacred Books of the East*).

A same-corpus ablation quantifies what the cleaning step removes. Without apparatus and length exclusion the identical passage set yields 29,048 candidate pairs, against 9,428 in the published cleaned run, a 67.5% reduction; because each configuration refits its own vectorizer, part of that reduction is vocabulary, IDF and neighbour-cap behaviour rather than exclusion, and holding the unfiltered fit fixed leaves 10,689 pairs between eligible passages. The two runs are not nested, so the decomposition is reported as approximate. Cleaning also has a measured cost: seven of the 95 quoted evidence fragments in the annotation set — the Popol Vuh wooden-people events and two Ymir body events — fall on passages the cleaning rules exclude. The narratives are in the corpus, but retrieval cannot reach the windows the coder actually cited, so cleaning must be described as reducing candidates sharply while its narrative recall remains unvalidated. Order diagnostics are unchanged in kind from the baseline: word-shuffled self-comparison retains 0.7125 mean similarity and sentence-shuffled self-comparison retains 0.9858, while a bigram-only model falls to 0.1071 under word shuffling. Lexical relevance therefore encodes local wording and is nearly blind to discourse order. Exploratory keyword counts make the same point about motif frequency: serpent references appear in 2,296 passages and storm/lightning in 2,033, but creation-from-body in only 16 and water-release in 4. Distributional inference is impossible for the rare tags at this corpus size.

The 9,428 passage pairs aggregate to 201 work pairs, 152 of which are not known dependent. The graph exposes 60 work nodes and 149 edges: the top 100 not-known-dependent work pairs plus all 49 configured-dependence controls, so the picture is a sampled triage list rather than a complete similarity map.

What the strongest surviving candidates actually are matters more than their count. The highest-ranked pair not known dependent, at 0.5166, joins *Myths of the Cherokee* and *Creek Migration Legends*; manual inspection found a repeated deer-hunting song opposite Hitchiti dialect material, not a shared event sequence. Second-ranked at 0.4994 are two Lewis Spence title pages. Third at 0.4874 pairs two Australian ethnographic works. Ninth, at 0.3123, rests on a **single** passage pair between an Iroquois collection and a South African folklore volume — an artifact of the maximum in \(R_{work}(A,B)=\max_{p\in A,q\in B}R_{lex}(p,q)\), which rewards long works and treats one incidental coincidence as a work-level claim. The candidate set also contains same-tradition pairs that no dependence rule covers, such as the *Poetic Edda* and *Völsunga saga* (122 passage pairs, 0.3886), where similarity is real and historically uninteresting. After 92% of raw candidates were removed as known reuse, what remained was still dominated by apparatus, formulaic material, collector overlap and single-pair noise. That is the empirical case for treating lexical retrieval as candidate generation only.

Every raw file hash, every byte-exact clean slice, all 34,281 passage offsets and word counts, all dependence labels and all graph endpoints and orientations are re-verified by `tools/validate_results_v2.py`, which also refuses to certify a graph built from stale inputs. Re-running the retrieval reproduced the 9,428-pair candidate file byte-for-byte, so the published ranking is a property of the configuration rather than of one execution.

### 5.8 Structural comparison of annotated narrative versions

The lexical pipeline can retrieve and filter, but it cannot say whether two narratives are structurally alike. That required a second engine operating on coded events rather than on words. A pilot annotation set was built for exactly this purpose: 14 narrative versions in four comparison classes (flood survival, storm-and-monster combat, cosmogony from a body, wisdom transfer), 94 events carrying 218 participant role assertions, 76 causal links, 38 preregistered motifs asserted 78 times, and 87 symbol assertions, with every event bound to a passage offset and each of 95 quoted fragments machine-verified against the corpus text it cites. Four versions rest on fragmentary attestations and are marked partial.

Similarity is decomposed rather than blended. An event pair receives

\[
e(a,b)=\frac{0.50\,p(a,b)+0.30\,r(a,b)+0.20\,o(a,b)}{\text{weights of measurable parts}},
\]

where \(p\) credits predicate identity (1.0 exact, 0.5 same predicate family, 0 otherwise), \(r\) is a maximum-weight bipartite matching over participants scored on the coder's asserted referent identity (1.0 same role and referent, 0.5 same role and referent kind, 0.25 same role different kind, 0 for a role mismatch) normalised by the larger participant set, and \(o\) is lexical overlap of coded outcomes. A part that cannot be measured on both sides drops out of the average instead of contributing a zero, so that incomplete coding is not read as narrative difference. Event sequences are then aligned by Needleman–Wunsch with mismatch cost \(1-e(a,b)\) and the preregistered gap \(g=1.0\), giving

\[
S_{sequence}=1-\frac{D_{mn}}{\max(m,n)}.
\]

Motif comparison is a weighted Jaccard restricted to the assessed domain: both versions must have adjudicated the motif, which for a full attestation means its comparison-class checklist and, for a fragmentary one, only what the surviving text actually reaches. Cross-class pairs return `NA`, not a low score. Causal, role and symbol components are computed the same way, and the symbol component reports which of its matched terms were already counted as motifs so that double counting is visible rather than silent.

The results are worth stating exactly, because they cut against the expectation that a good structural model "finds" famous parallels.

Four findings follow.

**Dependent witnesses separate cleanly from unrelated pairs.** Both known-reuse pairs outrank every predicted-unrelated pair on \(S_{sequence}\) (minimum 0.632 against maximum 0.045), and the Mahābhārata internal doublet anchors the scale at 1.000 across all five components. The Taliesin pair is more informative than a perfect control: the abridged *Celtic Myths* rendering drops three drops of grace, the year and a day and the blind fire-tender, and the motif domain falls from 11 to 5 while the event skeleton still aligns at 0.632. The engine measures what the retelling actually kept.

**Translation vocabulary does not enter the score.** Re-coding every participant name with a random alias changes \(S_{sequence}\) by 0.0 on all 91 pairs, because matching reads the asserted referent, not the translator's word. This is a property no lexical model has: the same rename destroys TF-IDF similarity. It also means the engine will not equate Thor with Indra merely because both are storm gods; they are different asserted referents, and only role and kind structure is shared.

**Event order, as preregistered, barely contributes.** Permuting one sequence lowers \(S_{sequence}\) by only 0.0186 on average, below the 0.02 floor fixed in advance, and just 5 of 91 pairs exceed \(p \leq 0.05\) against a 200-draw permutation null. The cause is arithmetic rather than a property of myth: with mismatch cost \(1-e \in [0,1]\) and \(g=1.0\), aligning is never more expensive than gapping both events, so 91 of 91 pairs match every event of the shorter sequence and the alignment behaves like a maximum-weight order-preserving matching. Measured directly against the crossing relaxation, order costs 0.0131 on average and 0.101 at most, and 77 of 91 pairs pay something for it. The honest conclusion is that \(S_{sequence}\) is an *event-matching* score, not a plot-order score. Restoring order sensitivity would mean lowering the gap, which imports a free parameter, moves the ranking (mean absolute rank shift 0.23–0.29 across the gap sweep), and changes what the measure means. The result is reported instead of tuned, and the motif component is exactly invariant under the same permutations, as the decomposition requires.

**Structure and wording carry different information.** Across the 78 pairs with both measures, Spearman \(\rho = 0.264\) between \(S_{sequence}\) and the lexical cosine, which is a maximum over the passage windows the annotation actually cites for the two versions rather than a whole-work maximum (it remains a maximum, and versions cite uneven numbers of windows, so a small bias in the same direction survives), reported as an exploratory association rather than a significant one. The textbook \(p = 0.019\) assumed 78 independent pair observations, which they are not: the 91 pairs share 14 versions, so every version contributes 13 correlated scores. A quadratic-assignment permutation test that holds the pair structure fixed and resamples only the version labels (200 draws, seed recorded in the output) gives two-tailed \(p = 0.065\). The direction of the association is what this pilot can support; its magnitude is not established, and no significance claim attaches to it. Noah–Manu scores 0.243 structurally against 0.0358 lexically; Noah–Gilgamesh scores 0.550 on adjudicated motifs over a domain of 4 against 0.095 on events; and Noah–Popol Vuh produces a structural comparison at all only because the annotation, not the retriever, brought them together. A ranking built on either measure alone would misrepresent the other.

Robustness checks are correspondingly modest, and their scale now matters. Rank movement is reported as mean absolute rank displacement divided by the full ranking range (\(P-1\)), so 0 is the published order and 1 is its exact reversal, with Kendall \(	au\) alongside as ordering agreement. Judgement weights barely move anything: the same-family predicate credit sweep gives shifts of 0.001–0.011 (\(\tau \geq 0.975\)) and the corpus-derived leave-one-region-out weights at most 0.017. The motif weight cap sweep (computed against the \(S_{motif}\) ordering over the 25 pairs with an assessed motif domain) moves at most 0.013 (\(\tau \geq 0.973\)). The gap penalty is another matter: 0.291 at \(g = 0.5\) (\(\tau = 0.106\)), 0.262 at 0.75, 0.231 at 1.25 and 0.284 at 1.5, against 0.001 at the preregistered \(g = 1.0\). The ordering is therefore parameter-bound on the alignment settings, not on the analyst's weights — which is the more worrying kind of dependence, because the alignment is where the measure's meaning is decided. A thin-domain audit flags 3 pairs whose motif score rests on a single adjudicated motif and 8 on two or fewer; those are reported as computed but should not be read as agreement. The engine emits no composite and no historical claim, and the suite's 37 structural-engine tests enforce that, including a brute-force cross-check of the alignment dynamic program, traceback-replay assertions (one of which found a real bug that mislabelled matched events while leaving every published score correct), the NA semantics of unassessed motifs, referent-only role matching, the recomputation of the published permutation \(p\)-value, the vocabulary boundary that keeps an unmarked pair from being called independent, and the absence of any composite field.

The limits of this pilot are as important as its results. All 14 versions were coded by a single analyst, so no inter-coder reliability estimate exists and the predicate families, motif definitions and coding decisions were authored by the same person who applied them. Quote verification proves that a fragment occurs in the cited passage; it does not prove the coding is correct. Four classes and 14 versions cannot support any distributional claim about world narrative, and the corpus they draw on is English translation. The engine is therefore a working instrument for measuring descriptive resemblance between coded narratives, and a test of which assumptions a structural claim can rest on. It is not, and does not claim to be, an ancestry estimator.

## 6. The limits of global reconstruction

Comparative reconstruction is strongest where languages, textual histories, and historically documented relationships provide independent constraints. It becomes weaker as comparisons move beyond those constraints. A shared name or formula within a demonstrably related language family has a different evidentiary status from a broad functional parallel between unrelated traditions. Likewise, a uniquely shared sequence has more diagnostic value than a common theme, but only if the alignment is not manufactured by flexible coding.

Three limits follow. First, the survival of evidence is uneven. Literate and archaeologically visible traditions are overrepresented, while many oral traditions are known through relatively recent records. Second, narrative elements do not behave as independent data points. Stories are copied, translated, performed, revised, and combined; a simple tree may therefore be less appropriate than a network or hybrid model. Third, some historical processes are observationally equivalent in surviving evidence. A shared feature may be compatible with both inheritance and an undocumented contact event, and current data may not distinguish them.

Independent invention must also be treated carefully. It is not proved merely because researchers have failed to find a route of transmission. It becomes better supported when the features in question recur under comparable conditions, lack specific shared innovations, and have distributions incompatible with the proposed historical route. Conversely, the absence of a known route does not make convergence certain.

The global single-origin hypothesis is especially vulnerable to unfalsifiability if every difference is classified as loss and every similarity as inheritance. A defensible model must specify in advance which observations would favor multiple families, transmission, or convergence instead. If it can explain every outcome equally well, it has no comparative predictive power.

Witzel’s globally comparative reconstruction is an important ambitious precedent, but its proposed large-scale groupings must be evaluated as contested historical hypotheses rather than accepted as a reference phylogeny (Witzel 2012).

These limits do not make global comparison useless. They change its function. Broad comparison can identify candidates, estimate motif distributions, and reveal recurring narrative solutions. Source-intensive regional investigation can then test historical explanations. The strongest claims are likely to concern particular families and episodes rather than an original narrative system for all humanity.

### 6.1 The motive claim, stated so that it can fail

This project was commissioned with a stronger hope than the analyses above test: that “God” is one invented concept — a single fiction that spread, rather than a response to anything real — and that its apparent worldwide recurrence is the evidence of its invention. That sentence contains three propositions with three different epistemic statuses, and separating them is the condition of testing them rather than a refusal to test them.

**Proposition A: divinity-narratives descend from a limited number of ancestral formulations.** This is a historical claim and is testable with the instruments in this paper. Its falsifiers are the ones the protocol preregisters: a shared *specific* complex (arbitrary, non-functional detail, not merely a shared theme) distributed along a dated contact chain; phylogenetic signal that survives a regional-null comparison; and the absence of the same complex from the earliest strata of families that had no contact route. Its disconfirmers are equally concrete: independent attestation in lineages with no plausible route, distribution patterns better predicted by convergence, and shared features that turn out to be artefacts of the translators’ English rather than of the source texts.

**Proposition B: the concept itself, not only its narratives, has a single origin.** This requires evidence that similarity scoring cannot supply: dated first attestations of each divine-concept grammar, the transmission of *doctrinal* vocabulary rather than plot, and an account of how a concept survives translation between languages whose grammatical subject positions differ. It is a legitimate research question for philology and the history of religion; this project’s apparatus is not yet addressed to it.

**Proposition C: no such beings exist.** No textual comparison can establish this or its negation. An idea’s origin tells you nothing about its truth: arguing from “this concept was narrated by humans” to “therefore it refers to nothing” is a genetic fallacy, and the reverse inference is no better. A project designed to prove C would necessarily read the same evidence as confirming when it favours the preferred conclusion, which is precisely the unfalsifiability condemned above. C is therefore excluded from this project’s scope by declaration, not by dismissal.

Proposition A has a precondition that nobody in this research tradition appears to have tested computationally, and this paper tests it: that the word *god* picks out one thing across the traditions being compared. `tools/divinity_terms.py` matches ten analyst-authored divine-concept grammars — personal creator of world, supreme-being attributes, plural or council deities, celestial bureaucracy, impersonal ground, deified human or ancestor, spirit immanent in things, saving descent, explicit non-theism or skepticism, and exclusivist monotheism — against the 8.73 million eligible words of the same 65-work corpus, recording every match with its passage and character span. The descriptive result stands and is reported here: no grammar is attested in all 65 works. The broadest is divinity-as-plurality (4,120 term hits, 50 works, 13 of 14 regional labels), followed by the personal creator (728 hits, 41 works) and supreme-being attributes (802 hits, 35 works); the impersonal absolute appears in 26 works, the deified-human grammar in 14, and heaven-as-bureaucracy in 3 works with 4 hits. Individual works carry between one and eight grammars.

**What that distribution does and does not establish, and this is the paper's correction to its own earlier conclusion.** It establishes that the English vocabulary of these translations is unevenly distributed across several distinguishable concepts. It does not establish that "the concept of God" has no stable referent, and it does not weaken an inheritance hypothesis, for two reasons that §6.1 of the previous draft did not face. First, the test chosen — must one grammar occur in *every* work? — is a universality test, and universality is not what historical inheritance predicts. Inheritance predicts descent with modification, and the modifications are themselves named: abstraction turns a personal creator into an impersonal ground, demotion turns a god into a demon or an ancestor, nominalisation turns a title into a proper name, silencing removes a term in a recension. A hypothesis that expects transformation expects the surface forms to diverge, so demanding that they not diverge refutes a straw version of it. Second, and more fundamentally, every instrument here detects an English surface pattern in one translation. Non-detection licenses exactly one report — "not detected in these sources" — and all four competing models (single origin with transformation, multiple independent origins, regional inheritance with mixing, independent development with convergence) predict that same non-detection pattern. An observation that all models predict cannot rank any of them. The census is therefore a description of translators' vocabulary, not evidence about ancestry, and the earlier sentence drawing a negative conclusion about common origin from it is withdrawn. [TRACK_B_METHOD_v2.md](../../TRACK_B_METHOD_v2.md) §8 states the inference rules that replace it, and §3 of that document replaces the ten conflated grammars with twelve orthogonal dimensions read proposition by proposition.

More pointedly, the one grammar that most closely resembles the motive claim’s content — the doctrine that divinity is literally one, expressed as “no god but …”, “beside me there is no God”, “the Lord our God is one” — was reported as documented in 46 passages drawn from four works (the King James Bible, the Qur’an, the Book of Mormon, and a nineteenth-century anthology of eastern scripture), and that result has now been audited match by match in [results/exclusivist_monotheism_audit.json](../../results/exclusivist_monotheism_audit.json), which publishes for each match its passage, its span as actually printed, the part heading and section it falls under, the composition it belongs to, the dependence rule that covers it, the sentence it was judged from, and the stated reason for its verdict. It does not survive at the weight the earlier paragraph gave it. All nine hits in the anthology volume resolve, from that volume’s own printed contents page and part headings, inside `SELECTIONS FROM THE KORAN`: eight sit in its reprinted Qur’an translation — a rendering of the same composition this corpus already counts, so they add no independent witness — and the ninth is the watchman’s cry “There is no God but God” as Thomas Carlyle reports it, in the essay the volume prints under his own byline, which is evidence that the formula circulated rather than the confession itself. `config/source_dependence.json` already carries the rule `quran_sale ↔ sacred_books_east = anthology_reuse`, so the census counted one source twice and reported it as two independent traditions, and the `Multiple` tradition label it attached to the anthology is an artefact of an anthology sitting beside the work it reprints. The audit had to correct the provenance in the other direction too: the file this corpus calls `quran_sale` prints `TRANSLATED FROM THE ARABIC BY THE REV. J.M. RODWELL` on its title page, so the work id, the acquisition manifest’s caution string and the dependence rule’s rationale all name the wrong translator; the anthology excerpt and this file are two different English renderings of one Arabic composition, which is why their verbatim overlap is nil and why independence had to be tested rather than read off a label. Two further hits are substring collisions that state nothing about divine oneness: 1 Corinthians 6:17, “he that is joined unto the Lord is one spirit”, and 1 Nephi 10:19, “the course of the Lord is one eternal round”, both produced by the pattern `the lord is one`; the second is the Book of Mormon’s only match, so that work ceases to be attested here at all. Three more are prohibitions on acknowledging or worshipping other gods, whose wording grammatically presupposes the gods they forbid, so they cannot witness to an existence claim even where they are scripture the text endorses. The count also under-reports its own formula class: because the raw Gutenberg files hard-wrap lines — Isaiah 44:6 is stored as “beside me there is no␤God” — the single-space patterns missed it, and normalising whitespace raises the total from 46 to 51, recovering Isaiah 44:6, Mark 12:29 and four Qur’an confessions; and a probe of 16 further oneness formula strings that the family never carried (“there is none else”, “none else beside”, “there is but one god”, “one without a second” among them) finds 79 occurrences, none of them caught by the published patterns. Those are deliberately *not* folded into the family, because widening a regex without this match-level adjudication reproduces the error that made the count untrustworthy; they are the measured size of the recall gap, and 51 is a floor. Finally, the identical first-person formula occurs in a non-divine mouth: Isaiah 47:8 and 47:10 put “I am, and none else beside me” in the mouth of Babylon, and Zephaniah 2:15 puts “I am, and there is none beside me” in the mouth of the rejoicing city, both of which the text is condemning — the same words carrying the opposite fact, indistinguishable to a term matcher, and all five of those formula hits, over those three verses, lie inside the 79 occurrences the family misses. The audited residue is 34 confessional matches in 2 independent works across 2 traditions, not 46 matches in 4 works across 4.

What remains after the audit is still real and still worth stating: exclusivist oneness is unevenly distributed across this corpus, concentrated in Abrahamic textual families, and absent from most works in it. But it is now a *descriptive* observation about a partly-audited vocabulary census, not a demonstration that the doctrine is confined to one family, and not a refutation of anything. If the motive argument borrowed the doctrine as evidence for a single invented concept it would still be circular — that part of the earlier reasoning stands, since it turned on the structure of the argument rather than on these counts. Texts that deny or suspend gods are also present — 116 machine matches in 20 works across ten tradition labels, published with locators and marked `unreviewed_machine_match` — though their reading requires care, since a conditional in the *Iliad*, the doxographic “atheists” of the Mahābhārata, and Isaiah’s “beside me there is no God” are three different speech acts. Forty-one monotheistic-formula matches were excluded from that family by a published rule and are printed rather than discarded, because a regex that cannot tell confession from denial must not be allowed to silently choose. The rebuilt frame handles those three speech acts as values on explicit interpretation dimensions — who speaks, what act, what genre, what commitment — rather than as exclusions bolted onto one regex family, and it retains rather than discards the record whose endorsement is withheld.

What this instrument can support is therefore narrower than the previous draft claimed, and it is descriptive rather than negative. The single-fiction hypothesis as originally phrased is under-specified: it needs a stated unit of analysis, and “the concept of God” as a single English word is not one. What it does not support is the inference the earlier version of this section made — that because no grammar is detected in every work, a common ancestry of divine concepts is impossible or discredited. That inference is withdrawn: absence of an English-language grammar in a one-translation corpus is an absence of a translator’s word, and because every competing model predicts that same absence, it carries no evidence about ancestry in either direction. The remaining limitations are equally stated: the probe was a single analyst’s lexicon without a second coder; attestation counts are undated, so they cannot order the grammars historically; and the original frame conflated conceptual dimensions in ways the audit exposed. The probe is a first descriptive pass whose purpose is to make the motive claim testable. Its replacement — a twelve-dimension, coverage-declared, proposition-level frame that is multilingual-ready rather than multilingual, with a blinded two-coder packet, a measured rather than asserted graph coverage figure, and four competing inheritance models whose predictions are separated before any count is quoted — is specified in [TRACK_B_METHOD_v2.md](../../TRACK_B_METHOD_v2.md), its own measurements are reported in Section 6.2 below, and Section 12.4 of the protocol is revised to point at it.

### 6.2 The rebuilt instrument: scale, coverage, and what it declines to assert

The replacement for the census is a state coder, and every one of its design decisions came from an external audit of the first version rather than from a result it produced. Seven changes matter for reading any number it publishes.

**The unit is a proposition, not a passage.** Each utterance window is split at coordinating and subordinating connectors; fragments under three words merge into the preceding proposition; a match straddling a boundary widens its proposition instead of being attributed to the wrong half. The run emits 47,641 records over 36,775 propositions in 34,584 windows within the same 23,385 eligible passages. Ninety-seven propositions are flagged as probable run-ons. Where the machine would have to choose between two values on a single-valued dimension it now publishes the conflict and demotes the record rather than resolving it by silent precedence — 2 conflicts on acknowledged population, 3 on speaker attribution. The segmentation is a machine approximation with four documented failure modes, one of them that Hebrew and Old Norse narrative joins clauses with no connector at all; a coder who re-segments a proposition produces a measurement of the segmentation, not an error to be corrected in the record.

**The composite dimensions were split.** Plurality became three questions — how many beings a proposition acknowledges, whether the others are real, whether worship of them is permitted — because one sentence can acknowledge other gods, treat them as real and forbid worshipping them, and a single "polytheism" score erased exactly that distinction. Human interpretation became four — speaker attribution, speech act, discourse genre, epistemic commitment — because a hymn can carry doctrine, a conditional can carry reported speech, and an opponent can make a theological argument. The frame is twelve dimensions carrying 93 declared values: 53 of them have an English pattern, 50 produced at least one record in this corpus, 40 have no detector at all, and 9 are published as not yet instrumented. Every dimension carries an `undecidable` residual, so a gap reads as the instrument's limit rather than as evidence of absence.

**Three questions stay separate.** `conceptual_presence`, `doctrinal_endorsement` and `historical_transmission` are three fields, not one state. The 54 matches the previous version refused outright — conditionals, questions, an opponent's reported speech, a claimant's boast — are retained as conceptual evidence whose endorsement is withheld: 47,262 records answer "represented in passage" and 379 answer "undecidable from available sources", while endorsement splits into 22,818 affirmed by the voice the text itself endorses, 9,211 attributed but not endorsed, 8,171 undecidable, 4,697 non-assertive, 1,575 suspended or held open and 1,169 rejected in the text. `counts_as_affirmative_doctrine` is the only field admissible for a doctrinal tally, and it is machine-classified, not human-verified. `historical_transmission` is `not_yet_assessable` in all 47,641 records, because `date_earliest`, `date_latest` and `dating_basis` are unset in every one.

**Scale is published as matrices, with a floor on interpretation.** The passage-by-value matrix holds 23,385 rows against 50 value columns, 1,169,250 cells; the work-by-dimension table holds 780 cells and now carries the candidate count and the affirmative-doctrine count as separate columns so a doctrinal tally can never exceed the candidates it is drawn from. A dark cell is not an absence in a tradition: it is either no candidate in this one English translation or a value the instrument cannot see, and those are different failures.

**Candidate generation is measured, not claimed.** The graph ships every pair the MinHash banding emitted — 538,529 passage-pair edges over the 14,036 passages carrying live states, aggregating to 1,949 work pairs, against a naive passage-pair space of 98,497,630 — where the previous build published 149 edges chosen by a top-100 truncation. "Every generated candidate was written" is still not "every possible pair was evaluated": banding never compares passages whose signatures agree in no band, so a validator now measures the loss directly. On a deterministic stratified subset of 1,632 of the 14,036 live passages (238 strata, small strata kept whole, otherwise the first eight passage ids in sorted order, no random selection and no fitted parameters), exhaustive comparison finds 413,552 pairs sharing at least one coded state; the published configuration recovers 9,600 of them, a measured recall of 0.0232, losing 48,043 to the bucket cap and 355,909 to banding itself, and lifting to 0.139 if the cap is removed. No pair reaches the edge file that does not actually share a state. The published edge set is therefore a blocking sample and is labelled `approximate_minhash_lsh_banding` with coverage `INCOMPLETE`; layer 4, inheritance and transformation, remains empty by design because dates are not data yet.

**The interpretation dimensions are drawn on their own denominator.** D7a–d are decided from the shape of an utterance window before any divine term is read, so a percentage on them is not comparable to a candidate count on a concept dimension. The visualization gives the four of them separate panels, a separate denominator of the 47,641 term-carrying records, their own legend, and a distinct caption saying so; the earlier chart had substituted a count of windows-read-as-doctrine into a heat map of matched candidates, which is the error this section exists to avoid repeating.

**The frame is multilingual-ready, not multilingual.** Its dimensions, values, operator vocabulary and evidence requirements are stated independently of English, and every record carries an `expression_language` field set to `en`; but all detectors in this run are English translation-text patterns, and no source-language detector is operational. Describing it as a language-independent instrument would claim the property the audit said it lacks.

Two artifacts complete the picture, and both are honest about being unused. A blinded packet of 4,699 propositions is ready for coding: 48 dimension-value strata targeted at 60 propositions each with 11 rare strata taken as whole-population censuses, all 65 works at 35 propositions each, all 8 speech acts at 45 each, seed 20261101 published, row order randomised so the oversampling of rare values is invisible, the coder view carrying no machine value and no dimension name, and the answer key kept in a separate file joined only once both codings exist. That is 56,388 judgments per coder, sized so a stratum of n = 60 puts a worst-case ±0.124 interval on its precision. The scorer exists and refuses to run on one coder or on the same sheet supplied twice; no human has coded the packet, so no reliability artifact exists in this repository and this paper reports no κ, no precision and no recall for the coder-facing instrument. The model-comparison layer likewise prints `not_yet_testable` with two missing inputs — no candidate derivation record, and 0 of 65 works carrying a filled composition range — while its operator cost table checks against a preregistration hash recorded when it was written, so a derivation's price cannot be edited after the result is known.

No number in this section licenses a historical claim, and that is its point. The instrument says how many propositions carry which coded state in one generation of English translations, which of those the text itself endorses, which values it cannot see, and what fraction of the pairs it could have compared it actually did. Whether any of those states travelled is a question about dated witnesses and typed routes, and the layer that would answer it is the one this build leaves empty.

## 7. Research implications

A productive global project should proceed from evidence-rich regional studies toward broader comparison, not from a universal genealogy toward confirming examples. Computational retrieval can manage scale, but it cannot replace textual criticism, historical linguistics, or regional expertise. Graphs can display disputed relationships, but the evidential status of every relationship must remain visible.

Future work should therefore prioritize three outputs: a passage-linked corpus of versions, independent annotations with reliability measures, and preregistered comparisons with controls. Reconstruction attempts should be limited to families whose relationships survive source criticism and alternative models. Negative results and unresolved cases must remain in the published corpus to prevent a file-drawer composed entirely of striking parallels.

For living traditions, scholarly access is not the only requirement. Community authority, consent, restrictions, and appropriate representation must shape collection and publication. These obligations are also methodological: removing a narrative from its performative and social context can destroy the evidence needed to interpret it.

Four gates stand between the present instruments and any historical conclusion. First, a second coder must independently annotate the same versions from the cited passages, because the predicate families, motif definitions and comparison classes used above were authored by the person who applied them, and no reliability estimate exists; `independently_annotated` is a review state in the schema that no version has yet reached. The identical condition applies to the state coder of Section 6.2, whose blinded 4,699-proposition packet exists, is scored by a tool that refuses one coder, and has never been opened: every D7 assignment, every affirmative-doctrine count and every speech-act split in this paper is single-analyst and machine-classified. Second, adjudicated historical edges must be produced one at a time by source criticism — the adjudication layer of both exported graphs is deliberately empty, and only a claim with dated witnesses, a stated route typed as descent or contact, and reviewed counterevidence may enter it. Third, retrieval must extend beyond English translation, since the dependence filtering of Section 5.7 partly measures the publishing history of nineteenth-century editions, and original-language or transliterated material changes which overlaps are informative; the detector sets for Sanskrit, Pali, Hebrew, Greek, Arabic, Akkadian and Chinese are the work that turns a multilingual-ready frame into a multilingual one. Fourth, the corpus must be balanced by independent narrative lineages rather than by work count; sixteen South Asian works against one Polynesian collection is a sampling design, not a distribution.

## 8. Conclusion

Narrative motifs demonstrably recur, but recurrence is not one historical phenomenon. Common descent, borrowing, convergence, and mixed development can all produce resemblances, and different elements of a story may have different histories. The source-critical pilot illustrates the descriptive differences concealed by a broad category such as “storm god and serpent,” and the instrumented stages that follow show what happens when those categories are operationalised rather than asserted.

Three instruments returned results that constrain the project's own hopes. Lexically, 92.0% of the candidate pairs in a 65-work corpus join works already known to depend on one another, so a similarity ranking built before source criticism mostly ranks the history of printing and translation; excluding editorial apparatus and undersized passages removes roughly two-thirds of the candidate set, although the same validation that certifies the reduction shows seven annotated evidence passages falling on excluded windows, so cleaning's narrative recall is itself an open question. Structurally, an event-alignment engine that is invariant to translator vocabulary and that separates its five components reproduces the expected ordering for dependent witnesses, but declines to reproduce the expected ordering for famous parallels: Noah–Gilgamesh scores 0.095 on events while scoring 0.550 on adjudicated motifs, and the sequence measure is nearly blind to event order for reasons of gap cost rather than reasons of myth. The composite score originally sketched in this project was abandoned because its components disagree, sometimes violently, and averaging them would have settled the argument before the evidence could be reported. Conceptually, the instrument that was built to test whether “god” names one thing worldwide first answered its own question badly — a universality test over English translation vocabulary, reported as though it bore on ancestry — and the corrected version answers nothing at all yet, because its inheritance layer is empty by design, its annotation packet has never been opened, and its model-comparison layer prints `not_yet_testable`.

The present evidence justifies continued investigation of particular narrative families. It does not justify a claim that all mythologies descend from one original system, and on current data it does not even license a distributional claim, since every structural result rests on single-analyst coding of 14 versions drawn from English translations. The scientifically valuable objective is therefore not to complete a universal tree at any cost, but to establish which relationships are supported, which are contradicted, and which are beyond recovery. An unresolved comparison is a valid result; an invented ancestor is not.

The project was begun in the hope that these recurrences would show the concept of a god to be one inherited fiction, and the honest report is what the instruments did with that hope. Decomposed into its three propositions, only the first is addressed by anything built here: that divine narratives share ancestry is a question the lexical and structural instruments can at least fail to answer cleanly; that the concept itself has one origin requires dated first attestations and evidence that doctrinal vocabulary, not plot, travelled, which this apparatus does not yet carry; and that no such being exists is not a claim any textual method can reach, treating an idea's human origin as its refutation being a fallacy that runs in both directions. Testing the precondition of the middle proposition — that “god” names one thing worldwide — the first divinity instrument reported ten concept grammars in the same 65 works with no grammar present in all of them, and reported the doctrine of strict oneness as documented in 46 passages across four works and four traditions. Neither report survives in the form it was given. The distribution is true of translators' vocabulary and nothing more, and the inference drawn from it — that no stable concept exists for an inheritance hypothesis to explain — is withdrawn; the oneness count, audited match by match, is 51 matches rather than 46 once Gutenberg's hard line-wraps are collapsed, its *Sacred Books of the East* hits sit inside a reprint of a Qur'an translation this corpus already counts, two are substring collisions, three are prohibitions whose grammar presupposes the gods they forbid, and what survives is 34 confessional matches in 2 independent works across 2 traditions. An observation that all four competing models predict equally cannot rank any of them, so this corpus yields no negative verdict about common ancestry — and, for the same reason, no positive one. The hoped-for conclusion is not disproved; it is untested. What the rebuild replaces it with is a machine that is capable of failing to give an answer on purpose and of saying so: twelve orthogonal dimensions read proposition by proposition, three separate questions per record, nine values declared undetectable, candidate coverage measured at 0.0232 instead of asserted as complete, a priced operator vocabulary with a path limit and a preregistration hash, and a comparison layer whose current verdict is `not_yet_testable` with the two missing inputs listed. What survives is the harder and more useful programme: dated attestation, original-language evidence, independent coding, and a claim-by-claim history of which divine ideas travelled, which converged, and which we cannot tell.
